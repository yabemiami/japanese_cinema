Some cinema components for talking about films
Three components we can see on the screen

When you like a movie, you just like it. But when we discuss films in a college course, we need structure — critical methods for thinking about them.

  • Mise-en-scène (setting-on-stage: how things are set)
  • Shots (how the camera captures scenes)
  • Editing (how shots are connected in a film)
Things we see & things we don’t see on the screen
  • The three components are elements we can see on the screen.
  • Cultural significance, historical background, and metaphorical interpretation are not physically on the screen; a filmmaker insinuates them by arranging visible elements, but they reside in the audience’s mind.
  • Let’s base our analysis on what the director presents on the screen — after all, we are WATCHING a movie.

Bordwell & Thompson call these the techniques of the medium: mise-en-scène, cinematography, editing, and sound — the concrete choices through which a film shapes meaning and feeling (FA, chs. 4–7).

Mise-en-scène (how things are set or appear)
Four areas of choice & control
  • Setting
  • Costume and makeup
  • Lighting
  • Staging: movement and performance

Bordwell: mise-en-scène (French, “putting into the scene”) names the director’s control over what appears in the frame — grouped into these four areas (FA, p. 114).

Mise-en-scène — setting & the power of the frame
From the reading — Bordwell, ch. 4
  • Setting can be built for authenticity or heavily stylized; it can even dominate the actors (FA, pp. 116–17).
  • A prop (a term borrowed from theater) can “activate” an area of the frame and guide the eye (FA, p. 117).
  • Color, too, is a component of setting — it can make a figure stand out or support a narrative shift (FA, p. 117).

Authenticity in constructed settings: posters and flypaper build a tavern in Greed (left); for All the President’s Men, even wastepaper from the real newsroom was scattered on the set (right) (FA figs. 4.10–4.11).

Mise-en-scène — lighting
From the reading — three-point lighting
  • Classical Hollywood used at least three sources per shot: the key light (primary, strongest shadows), the fill light (softens the key’s shadows), and the backlight (edge of brightness separating figure from ground) (FA, pp. 126–28).
  • High-key lighting = low contrast, few heavy shadows (comedies, bright dramas). Low-key lighting = hard light, strong shadows, chiaroscuro (film noir) (FA, p. 129).

The basic three-point setup: key (front-diagonal), fill (near the camera), and backlight (behind and above) (FA fig. 4.70).

Shots (how the camera captures scenes)
Elements — cinematography
  • The range of tonalities (exposure, brightness/darkness, color filters)
  • Speed of motion (number of frames; duration of an event)
  • Perspective (lenses, focus)
  • Framing (what is included / excluded)
  • The mobile frame (crane shots, dolly shots)
  • Duration of the image: the long take
Cinematography — the photographic image
From the reading — tonalities, exposure, color, speed
  • Tonal contrast guides the eye and sets mood: high-contrast is “stark and dramatic,” low-contrast “more muted” (FA, p. 160).
  • Exposure regulates light through the lens — the trade-off between a blown-out window and a silhouetted figure (FA, p. 162).
  • Speed of motion: filming faster than 24 fps yields slow motion on playback; slower yields fast motion (FA, ch. 5).

Tonal contrast: balanced grays in Casablanca (left) vs. near-white, high-contrast dream imagery in Bergman’s Wild Strawberries (right) (FA figs. 5.1–5.2).

Cinematography — the lens & the frame
From the reading — perspective, framing, duration
  • Focal length shapes perspective: wide-angle lenses stretch space and exaggerate depth; telephoto lenses flatten it, making planes seem stacked (FA, p. 164).
  • Depth of field is the range in focus; keeping foreground and background sharp is deep focus, a major option of the 1940s–50s (FA, p. 168).
  • Framing controls angle, height, distance (shot scale: long shot → close-up) and defines onscreen vs. offscreen space (FA, p. 186).
  • The mobile frame (pan, tilt, tracking, crane, zoom) and the long take let a shot unfold space and time without a cut (FA, pp. 196, 208).
Editing (how shots are connected in a film)
Four relations between shot A & shot B
  • Graphic relations (shape, color, composition)
  • Rhythmic relations (a rhythm across shots; shot length)
  • Spatial relations (shots that build a space)
  • Temporal relations (shots that order story time)
  • Continuity editing (the 180-degree imaginary line)

Bordwell organizes all editing along exactly these four dimensions (FA, p. 219).

Editing — the four relations up close
From the reading — Bordwell, ch. 6
  • Graphic match: shots linked by shared shape, color, or composition (smooth continuity or deliberate clash).
  • Rhythmic: shot lengths set a tempo — rapid cutting accelerates, long shots slow us down.
  • Spatial: editing builds space. An establishing shot can be broken into closer views (analytical editing) — or withheld, letting cuts assemble a space that was never filmed together (constructive editing, the Kuleshov effect) (FA, pp. 224–26).
  • Temporal: editing reorders story time — flashback/flashforward, and ellipsis (skipping story time) via cut, dissolve, fade, or wipe (FA, p. 228).

Dynamic graphic match: Kurosawa cuts six brief, graphically matched shots of running samurai in Seven Samurai (FA figs. 6.11–6.13).

Continuity editing & the 180° system
The axis of action & its devices
  • Space is built around the axis of action (the 180° line). Keeping the camera on one side preserves consistent screen direction (FA, p. 231).
  • Its devices: the eyeline match (A looks offscreen, B shows what is seen), the shot/reverse shot, and the match on action (a movement carried across the cut) (FA, p. 234).

Shot/reverse shot across the 180° line in The Maltese Falcon (FA figs. 6.61–6.62). We will see Ozu deliberately break this system.

Narrative Continuity
A term I might use: “narrative continuity”

Roughly, narrative is how a story is told — in the film, on the screen, and in the sound track. One story can have different narratives; how a filmmaker presents a story is one cinematic narrative, not the story itself. Plot usually refers to the ordering of events.

I take ‘narrative’ in cinema to include anything on the screen that does not directly form events within the story — unrelated images, colors, light or darkness, sounds. If even unrelated elements carry the narrative forward, the cinematic narrative continues to intrigue the audience.

From the reading — Bordwell, ch. 3 (narrative form)

Bordwell distinguishes story (all events in order) from plot (everything actually present in the film we watch), and calls the delivery of story information narration — which may be restricted to a character’s knowledge or freely omniscient, as in Citizen Kane. He also separates diegetic (story-world) from nondiegetic material (FA, ch. 3).

Narrative Continuity for Ozu
Example
  • In Ozu’s conversation sequences, the conventional continuity shot is disrupted by his obsession with uniformity of composition: each shot of conversing characters appears in a similar way. Ozu seeks compositional continuity rather than just storyline continuity.
  • Audiences are often story-centric. But freeing our minds for cinema viewing, we can sense continuities on different dimensions — perhaps even “bad” movies could be appreciated anew.
From the reading — Hasumi (2024)
  • For Ozu, what matters is not whether eyelines meet but “the mere fact that they extend in the same direction” — unidirectional looking (HS, p. 129).
  • His disregard of the 180° rule was a deliberate policy: his editor raised the issue, but Ozu did not change course (HS, p. 154).
  • Hasumi links this to a deeper claim: cinema can film the eye but never the look itself — the “cruel schism between the eye and the look” — and Ozu, rather than concealing this limit, exposes it (HS, p. 152). (See the supplement: “Ozu & the Imaginary Line.”)
