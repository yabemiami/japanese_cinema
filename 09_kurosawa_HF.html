Introduction
An entertainment that reshaped world cinema

The Hidden Fortress (1958) is Kurosawa’s first film in widescreen and one of his most purely entertaining: a fast, funny samurai adventure. A defeated clan’s princess and her general, in disguise, must smuggle the clan’s gold across enemy territory to safety, helped and hindered by two greedy peasants. Donald Richie called it “romantic,” “mythic,” “operatic,” and “fairy-tale” — then Kurosawa’s most expensive film, and his most financially successful.

  • Told from below: the story unfolds from the point of view of the two lowest characters, the bickering peasants Tahei and Matashichi.
  • A cinematic image: the tall one and the short one, a contrast Kurosawa turns into visual accent, rhythm, and comedy.
  • A long afterlife: by George Lucas’s own account, the single strongest influence on Star Wars — a debt this session traces in detail.
Kurosawa and Lucas
Lucas on Kurosawa and Hidden Fortress

“I had never seen anything that powerful or cinematographic. The emotions were so strong that it did not matter that I did not understand the culture or the traditions. From that moment on, Kurosawa’s films have served as one of my strongest sources of creative inspiration.” (Lucas, 2004)

“The one thing that really struck me about The Hidden Fortress was that the story was told from the perspective of the two lowest characters… That set me off on a very interesting course, that was the strongest influence for me in Star Wars, and it is what altered the point of view of all three movies.” (Bouzereau 1997)

Stories told by the two lowest characters

Hidden Fortress and Star Wars are both told through a pair of the socially lowest characters: the peasants Tahei and Matashichi, and the servant droids C-3PO and R2-D2 — who even share dialogue with the peasants, as when they curse their “lot in life” (Kaminski, p. 87).

The debt is documented. Lucas’s first Star Wars treatment (April 1973) “included every single scene from Hidden Fortress,” substituting a fourteen-year-old Princess Leia for Princess Yuki, a General Skywalker for General Makabe, and “a pair of bickering bureaucrats (later turned into robots named R2-D2 and C-3PO) for the comedic peasant duo Tahei and Matashichi” (Kaminski, p. 87). Having no home video, Lucas worked from Donald Richie’s 1965 plot summary, copying entire passages almost verbatim.

A cinematic image: the pair of the tall one and the short one
Three ways to read the pair

We can talk about this cinematic image in a few different ways, based on how the two fare on the screen:

  1. Narrative: a comic duo (tall and serious vs. short and funny).
  2. Sergei Eisenstein’s typage: an image shaped by a stereotype the audience already carries.
  3. Visual composition: the contrast as a graphic accent in the frame.
A contrasting comic duo

The contrasting comic duo is an old narrative device across writing, drawing, and manga. The contrast in the two bodies is transferred onto temperament or social role: tall / short → serious / funny; solemn / goofy.

  • Don Quixote and Sancho Panza (a richer, less single-note pairing).
  • Mutt and Jeff: a tall, serious gambler and a short, comedic, unpredictable sidekick.
  • Yaji and Kita from a Japanese humorous travelogue (from 1802): equally comic, yet in stage and print images Yaji is always drawn taller, Kita shorter.
Sergei Eisenstein’s typage
What is typage?

Typage is a technique that relies on actors’ physical characteristics to reflect the roles they play. Physical features are quickly associated with the class, profession, or social role of a character within the audience’s social context; a strong actor-role link can even lead to typecasting. Eisenstein built cinema from montage — effective sequences and patchworks of signs — and the signs include both visual images (through editing) and the audience’s mental images, such as typage. His style is often called the dialectics of film form.

Typage in Hidden Fortress and Star Wars
  • Taller Tahei is always the scheming one, trying to manipulate shorter Matashichi.
  • The more humanly shaped C-3PO is given human traits and a naive anxiety, while the “computer-in-a-case” R2-D2 is brave and stubborn where humans would be afraid.
How Kurosawa uses the Tahei–Matashichi duo on screen
The duo provides visual accents
  • Images: the contrast of their heights is translated directly onto the screen, giving immediate visual accent and rhythm whenever they are juxtaposed.
  • In the frame: the contrast can be worked through different camera angles and placements, letting Kurosawa capture space dramatically as the narrative develops within it.
  • Movements: the physical contrast accentuates the action — climbing a wall, long-legged Tahei races up while short-legged Matashichi struggles; fleeing with stolen rice and a pot, low-geared Matashichi dashes faster even as Tahei hauls the heavy sack.
What Lucas saw: Kurosawa’s visual style
“Mostly everything is visual”

Lucas is drawn less to Kurosawa’s stories than to his images. “I’m not known for my dialogue,” Lucas said; Kurosawa’s films “work because they speak to us at a direct, perhaps more primal, level… through images” (Kaminski, p. 89). “The visual graphics, and the framing and the quality of the images, goes a long way to telling the story and setting the mood” (p. 94).

A style forged from lenses and cameras
  • Telephoto lenses: long lenses flatten space into abstract, graphic patterns, stacking distant planes on top of near ones — moving the image “away from realism” toward composition (Kaminski, pp. 91–92).
  • Multi-camera shooting: because the action in Seven Samurai was too large to repeat, Kurosawa filmed with up to five cameras at once, which in turn required those long lenses — a technique that became his signature (p. 91).
  • Roots in silent cinema: Kurosawa grew up on silent film (his brother Heigo was a benshi), absorbing Eisenstein, Murnau, and above all John Ford — the westerns whose “grammar” he said he learned (pp. 90–91).
From Hidden Fortress to Star Wars: a scene-by-scene inheritance
The armature Lucas kept

Beyond the peasant duo and the hidden treasure (gold in Hidden Fortress; the Death Star plans inside R2-D2), Lucas carried over the whole shape of the tale: a princess on the run with her retainers and clan treasure, her protector-general (Makabe → General/Ben Kenobi), and a story framed from the servants’ point of view.

Lucas prized this above all: the film is “told from the point of view of the farmers, and not… the princess. It really did frame the movie” (Kaminski, p. 87). Producer Gary Kurtz even considered buying the rights from Toho. When Kurosawa heard Lucas was using his characters, he waved off any fee, smiled, and said, “use them all you like” (Nogami, in Kaminski, p. 89).

Specific borrowings across the original trilogy
Compositions lifted, sometimes shot for shot
  • The cantina brawl comes from Yojimbo: a rapid burst of cutting, a sword suddenly drawn, and an insert of a severed arm falling with the weapon still in hand — “transplanted… surprisingly faithfully” (Kaminski, p. 94).
  • The Millennium Falcon hiding place reworks a reveal from Sanjuro, with heads emerging from removable floorboards.
  • Wipes: a near-obsolete transition by the 1970s, which Kurosawa used “to great and aggressive effect” for changes of time and place — and Lucas revived.
  • The Kenobi–Vader duel “seems lifted in its entirety from Hidden Fortress” (Will Brooker): like the Makabe–Tadokoro staff fight, it opens on a wide profile tableau, moves through shot/reverse-shot, and climaxes in close-ups with the weapons crossing diagonally across the frame (Kaminski, p. 95).
  • The Return of the Jedi speeder chase restages the Hidden Fortress horseback duel — two warriors side by side before one is thrown headfirst; Lucas storyboarded it himself.
A villain’s redemption — and a face from Hidden Fortress
Tadokoro, Mifune, and the shape of Star Wars characters
  • Vader’s redemption in Return of the Jedi is prefigured in Hidden Fortress: the villain General Tadokoro, whose facial scar even resembles the unmasked Vader, turns on his own side and saves the heroes from execution (Kaminski, p. 88).
  • Ben Kenobi is modeled on Toshiro Mifune’s general; Lucas at one point pursued casting Mifune himself as Obi-Wan before the role went to Alec Guinness.
  • The wider debt runs through recurring Kurosawa themes Lucas absorbed: the master–pupil bond, the dichotomy of illusion and reality, class division seen “from below,” and the awakening of a hero (p. 89). Even the name “Jedi” echoes jidai-geki, the Japanese period film.
The prequels return to Kurosawa
The Phantom Menace: Lucas’s most Kurosawa-influenced film

Directing again after two decades, Lucas leaned hard on the master. He adopted Kurosawa’s late two-camera setup, and Phantom Menace — loosely a remake of Hidden Fortress, following its plot even more closely than the original Star Wars — favors the master shot, a feeling of stillness, and composition over crane or steadicam movement, “circa Ran” (Kaminski, pp. 95–96).

  • Droid tanks cresting a hillside echo the bandits emerging from the mountains in Seven Samurai; the rolling hills of Naboo recall the opening quarter of Ran.
  • The Gungan army advancing out of mist is an iconic image from Throne of Blood; the closing celebration is framed like the final shot of Hidden Fortress.
  • Liam Neeson’s Qui-Gon takes over Mifune’s role, dressed with a long ponytail and distinct facial hair.
The clone wars, Ran, and the break
Kurosawa’s battles — then Lucas finds his own style
  • Attack of the Clones stages its battles like the great clashes of Ran: color-coded forces and consistent screen direction (green/blue fire attacking right-to-left, red fire left-to-right), and armies firing through a wash of smoke. Its lovers in a field of flowers echo Seven Samurai (Kaminski, pp. 96–97).
  • Revenge of the Sith is nearly barren of Kurosawa — only a nod as Yoda strokes his head like Takeshi Shimura, and even that was added by an ILM animator, not Lucas. The mentor is finally set aside (p. 97).
  • A reverse metamorphosis: where Kurosawa moved toward telephoto formalism, Lucas moved the opposite way — from the abstraction of THX 1138 to the warm, Ford-like lenses of American Graffiti and beyond (p. 94).
