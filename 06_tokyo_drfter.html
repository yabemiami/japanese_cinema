Introduction
Seijun Suzuki and the pleasures of nonsense

Seijun Suzuki (1923–2017) turned the studio yakuza picture into pop-art spectacle. Tokyo Drifter (1966) follows a reformed gangster hunted through a stylized, color-saturated Tokyo. Suzuki cheerfully abandons the continuities we expect, chasing expressiveness over coherence.

  • “Time and place are nonsense”: the director’s own motto, and the title of Tom Vick’s study (Vick, p. 59).
  • Broken continuity: spatial and temporal logic are eschewed for a theatrical, beyond-the-stage style.
  • Film as material: the grainy opening was shot on expired stock—“film as a conveyor of illusions as well as a physical substance subject to decay” (Vick, p. 56).
  • A long shadow: his style echoes through global action cinema.
“Time and Space is nonsense”: rules are nonsense
Expected continuities of the plot are often violated by other elements

In Tokyo Drifter (and in many of his films), the continuity of plot, both spatial and temporal, that we expect from films is compromised or intentionally eschewed for Suzuki’s own style of expressiveness. It can be analyzed as having adopted elements from the theatrical style of stage plays, but Suzuki’s style seems to go beyond that. The very title of Tom Vick’s book on Suzuki, Time and Place are Nonsense, comes from a remark by the director himself: “In my films, time and place are nonsense” (quoted in Vick, p. 59).

  • It starts with black-and-white footage. Vick explains the texture: those grainy, high-contrast opening scenes were shot on expired film stock. Suzuki had no idea how they would come out. “The effect becomes unstuck from the narrative, emphasizing film as a conveyor of illusions as well as a physical substance subject to decay and manipulation” (Vick, p. 56).
  • Why is there a fence in an office?
  • How can there be a vast space in an underground music club?
Are films all about storytelling?
  • During his time at Nikkatsu, Suzuki was assigned to direct as many as four movies a year (Vick puts the quota at two to six). They are all B-class movies or pop movies, like Tokyo Drifter, which is a promotion film for the pop star Tetsuya Watari and his new pop song single. As Suzuki himself said of the singing-idol vehicle, “When you are using a song like that, the story ceases to matter” (Vick, p. 56). The plot is what Vick calls a MacGuffin, a way for Suzuki to bolt a series of bizarre set pieces to what is essentially an American Western (complete with saloon brawl).
  • Suzuki’s style was often criticized by the mainstream sector of the film industry in his time as eclectic, absurd, or even nonsensical. He was eventually fired.
  • Q: Do his approaches to cinematic expression get in the way of the expressiveness of his films?
Movies: Suzuki’s evasion of standstill
Suzuki shares the same challenge as others: film media has limitations (1)

What is shown or mentioned in a film must make sense in its entirety. But the cinematic reality itself is not our reality. “Cinema is truth 24 times a second, and every cut is a lie.” (Jean-Luc Godard) While cinema can reflect elements of reality, it ultimately constructs its own version of it: a world that operates by different rules from our own.

  • Films project a 3-D world onto a flat and limited square frame (space).
  • A film has a fixed projection time (e.g., 90 minutes) while the plot depicted within it usually spans a much longer interval.
  • Hasumi Shigehiko suggests that one of the truly valuable traits of Suzuki, before calling him postmodern or a pop artist, is his creativity in revealing the limitations of film media that all filmmakers have to face. He has very unique and effective styles to challenge those limitations and to conceal them (while leaving some traces for us). As Hasumi puts it directly: “the result of Suzuki’s continuous investigation of the film medium is that he finds out more about its limitations than about its possibilities” (quoted in Vick, p. 33). Vick adds a useful image: for Suzuki, the screen is “not merely a window onto an imaginary world, but a surface to play with” (Vick, p. 33).
Movies: Suzuki’s evasion of standstill (continued)
Suzuki shares the same challenge as others: film media has limitations (2)
  • Suzuki’s hyper-exaggeration (or hypo-nonchalance) regarding space and time, stretched beyond reasonable limits, makes us question what is accepted as proper cinematic representation. This creates a critical awareness in the viewer, revealing that film is never a true reflection of reality: Oh well, it’s just a film after all. Film is an institution where we collectively pretend to witness reality. Watching a film is like subconsciously signing a contract in which you agree to accept what you see as reality. The “contract” Suzuki makes you sign, however, is so bad or absurd that it becomes impossible to accept as reality. Instead, the impossibility makes you aware of the contract itself.
I myself have always made films for entertainment. I believe that film is a spectacle. Arousing the spectator’s curiosity, dressing up the unreal to make it look real, fooling the spectator, that’s what a spectacle is all about. The spectacle is a game in which you know from the start that you are being fooled, and not a bad word about the spectator who thinks the fake is real; the one who sees the falsehood for what it is does not get angry, but laughs about the nonchalance with which he has bought his ticket. — Seijun Suzuki, quoted in Vick, p. 33
  • Films, also called movies, are a form of media based on moving images. Suzuki keeps the action constantly moving on the screen, never allowing the audience to drift into thoughts that would create a standstill in their viewing experience of these moving images. It is as if he wants the audience to enjoy what they see (the movie, the moving images) without pausing to reflect on the background, keeping their focus on the continuous motion on the screen. You don’t reality-check Tom and Jerry’s fast chases. It would kill the enjoyment. It is just a cartoon, just as a movie is just a movie.
  • It is as if Suzuki keeps his cinematic space loose and relaxed, allowing images to move, shift, or change around easily and rapidly to compensate for his absurdity.
An interpretive aside: Lévi-Strauss on signs, opposition, and bricolage
Why bring in Lévi-Strauss here?

Suzuki’s most consistent move is to refuse the cultural meanings that ordinarily come welded to natural or visual material: red does not have to mean blood, winter does not have to mean sadness, snow does not have to feel cold. Claude Lévi-Strauss, the French anthropologist, gives us a precise vocabulary for what Suzuki is dismantling. (Vick’s book does not cite Lévi-Strauss; this is our overlay.)

Three Lévi-Straussian concepts useful here
  • Binary opposition. Cultures organize meaning through paired contrasts: nature / culture, raw / cooked, hot / cold, fresh / decayed, sacred / profane. It is the pair that carries meaning, not either pole alone (“The Structural Study of Myth,” 1955; The Raw and the Cooked, 1964).
  • Mytheme. The smallest meaning-bearing unit of a myth. A mytheme does not mean anything in itself; it means only in relation to the other mythemes around it. Think of it as the cinematic equivalent of a shot or a color-card: empty alone, charged in a sequence.
  • Bricolage (La Pensée sauvage / The Savage Mind, 1962). The bricoleur builds with whatever ready-made bits are at hand, not with purpose-engineered materials. A B-movie director, handed a recycled gangster script and a tight schedule, is the bricoleur par excellence. Vick’s description of Suzuki at Nikkatsu (“discarding inessential links in the narrative structures, judiciously exaggerating other elements of the story and decorating the whole with stylized colors and boldly theatrical visual effects”, Vick, p. 32) is almost a definition of bricolage.
Where this lands on Suzuki

When Vick reports that Suzuki “experimented with separating traditional seasonal and weather symbolism from its cultural grounding” (Vick, p. 33), he is, in structuralist terms, untying the nature / culture pairing that myth normally welds together: snow welded to cold, winter to death, red to blood, cherry blossoms to impermanence. Suzuki keeps the natural (or sensory, or visual) pole and discards the cultural pole.

In Lévi-Strauss’s vocabulary, ordinary mythic thought cooks the raw material of the world by binding it to a cultural code. Suzuki’s gesture is the reverse: he unbinds the cultural code and gives us the raw element on its own. The next three slides are easier to read with this in mind.

What we see in a moving, expanding, or passing Tokyo Drifter
Space: “Drifting” / horizontal expansions
  • While the surrounding space for a scene is never established, Suzuki avoids the architectural integrity of scenes and directs his characters to the outside space, letting their actions expand out of the screen into an unknown dimension.
  • His fugitive tour to the north starts with a scene in which trains move horizontally in the snow. It is as if a word in the title, drifter, comes from the fugitive sequences. Just like the train, Tetsu is chased and drifts around in the snow horizontally. The wide screen suits this approach very well.
Time: seasons are cinematic images

The sudden changes in natural phenomena, like snowfall or seasonal shifts, don’t give us time for introspection about the seasons or anything beyond what is on screen. Suzuki uses these elements purely as transitions (changes in scenery, color, or movement), turning them into purely cinematic events. In Tokyo Drifter, the snow doesn’t even seem cold.

Vick describes this as one of the deepest moves in Suzuki’s Nikkatsu phase: he “experimented with separating traditional seasonal and weather symbolism from its cultural grounding, as well as with creating his own weather symbolism, using such elements as unmotivated wind effects to illustrate his characters’ emotions” (Vick, p. 33). In Lévi-Straussian terms, Suzuki untwists the nature / culture pairing on which the conventional weather code rests, and hands us back the natural pole as bare material.

Colors: no meaning

Colors often dominate scenes. These colors don’t seem to carry any meaning or metaphor. They simply propel the continuity of the film by changing from one to another, or dramatize the intensity of the plot, without conveying any meaning. Tony Rayns captures it well: Suzuki brought tired genre material to life “by discarding inessential links in the narrative structures, judiciously exaggerating other elements of the story and decorating the whole with stylized colors and boldly theatrical visual effects” (quoted in Vick, p. 32). In structuralist vocabulary, his colors are signifiers that have been let loose from their signifieds, mythemes drained of myth.

Suzuki’s expressions are all complete on the screen
It’s all on the screen
  • It seems as if Suzuki keeps every part of his cinematic expression contained within the screen. The spaces off the screen are often undefined, in other words, dimensionless. As Vick puts it, more than a maverick bucking against his studio bosses, Suzuki was “a filmmaker bucking against the movie screen itself”, “a filmmaker dismantling cinema’s basic illusions to create a whole new set of his own” (Vick, p. 10).
  • He does not rely on cultural references or anything outside the film to convey ethos or meaning: red stands for blood; winter is sad and lonely. These two struck-through phrases are textbook binary oppositions in Lévi-Strauss’s sense, the kind of nature / culture welding that ordinary mythic thought performs. Suzuki simply refuses the cultural pole and keeps only the natural one on screen.
    • In Suzuki’s films, colors seem to be just a set of elements on a palette. Red is simply one color among many, without symbolic extension. Red is a signifier without its signified.
    • Chasing through snow accentuates movement, making it more cinematic. It has nothing to do with the symbolism of winter. Snow is white, fast, and pretty, no more.

Suzuki’s cinematic expressions and impressions seem to be entirely contained within the screen, maximizing his cinema as a collage of moving images: in Lévi-Strauss’s vocabulary, the work of a bricoleur who arranges what is at hand without pretending the arrangement adds up to a myth. The totality of these images in balance makes Suzuki’s movies eccentric yet original and admirable.

Influences and echoes (1)
Kanto Wanderer (1963)

A similar gangster / yakuza movie with a style close to Tokyo Drifter.

  • The widescreen format is effectively used. During the main fight scene, the protagonist Katsuta exits the screen from the left edge, re-enters from the right, slays his opponent, and throws him into the paper wall at the center of the screen. The wall falls, revealing a red backlight. Katsuta seems to warp through the void Suzuki leaves at the edges of his screen, without defining or giving it time or space.
  • This film contains images of seasons without the actual seasons. In one sequence, symbols of two different seasons are presented in the same set. Altogether, the seasons appear in the wrong order: fall, summer, then spring. Suzuki may be expressing the passage of time (at least two and a half years) by treating the seasons as turns of events: fall comes, summer comes, spring comes, but he does not invite us to expand on the seasonal images. Suzuki does not depict the incarcerated Katsuta against any of those seasons behind bars.
Influences and echoes (2)
Tattooed Life (1965)

A similar katana battle scene appears in Tattooed Life (1965). The famous full-screen shot of the two opponents facing each other, filmed from below through a transparent floor, comes from this film. Suzuki seems to take his use of color to center stage in this battle scene.

Worth noting for the next two slides: Nikkatsu told Suzuki he’d gone “too far” with Tattooed Life, and as “punishment” gave him a reduced budget for his next film, Tokyo Drifter (Vick, p. 55–56). And Vick documents that among Quentin Tarantino’s roughly twenty references to Japanese cinema in Kill Bill Vol. 1 is a direct nod to the see-through-floor shots in Tattooed Life (Vick, p. 165).

Suzuki’s shadow on global action cinema (1)
The John Wick series (2014–2023) by Chad Stahelski

The John Wick series incorporates many cinematic motifs from Suzuki. There is a frequently identified plot construction in which the protagonists initially hesitate but are eventually drawn into their mission (cf. The Hero with a Thousand Faces, Campbell, 1949). A loyal gangster, Tetsu, and a heartbroken assassin, John, are both violently pulled into conflict at the onset of their films, even though neither is particularly willing to fight. As their battles unfold, we can observe many shared characteristics in the battle sequences. You identify these shared features for me.

Vick’s wider claim sets the table: Suzuki is “a filmmakers’ filmmaker.” Directors are steeped in the rules of narrative filmmaking, so the thrill for them is in watching Suzuki “flagrantly break those rules”: inserting an invisible floor into a scene, making a hash out of a script’s plot in order to emphasize its nonsensicalness (Vick, p. 166).

Suzuki’s shadow on global action cinema (2)
Suzuki’s influences: Kill Bill (2003) by Quentin Tarantino

There are so many features in plot, mise-en-scène, shots, lighting, and so on, modeled after Japanese B-films (such as Lady Snowblood, 1974) in Kill Bill as homage. For the big fight sequence in the nightclub, much of the visual repertoire comes from Suzuki’s style. After watching the fight scenes from Kanto Wanderer (1963), Tattooed Life (1965), and Tokyo Drifter (1966), we can identify many features carried into the big night-club sequence.

Vick’s book lists the Tarantino borrowings, including a direct nod to the see-through-floor shot from Tattooed Life in Kill Bill Vol. 1, and credits Tarantino’s “sincere connoisseurship of trashy B-movie aesthetics” as the channel through which Suzuki’s sensibility reached a 1990s American audience (Vick, pp. 164–165).
