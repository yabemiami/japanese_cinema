‘The quietest scene by the sea from that summer’
Introduction
Kitano’s unconventional style

Kitano Takeshi’s direction is built on subtraction. He strips away dramatic convention and lets silence, stillness, and visual space carry what dialogue would ordinarily explain. The points below are the working vocabulary for this week; most of them are argued at length in a recent doctoral study of Kitano’s style, cited throughout as Vlasov 2024.

  • Aesthetics of subtraction: the decisiveness of eliminating explanatory shots, a refusal to fill narrative gaps with exposition.
  • Narrative fragmentation: the film has “no dramatic skeleton”; the life of this man, possessed by the sea, “consists of nothing but isolated episodes.” (Vlasov 2024, 21)
  • Indirect depiction of pivotal events: accident and death are not filmed directly. Shigeru’s death reaches us through a surfboard on the sand.
  • Sparse dialogue: roughly 280 lines in the whole film, against 1,000 to 1,700 in an ordinary drama. (Vlasov 2024, 25)
  • “Mannequin-like” acting: performers as Bresson’s models, impassive, which distances the character from society and from the film’s own world. (Vlasov 2024, 126)
  • Ontological solitude: isolation is not caused by the plot; it is given from the first frame, whether the character is alone or among friends. (Vlasov 2024, 125–126)
  • Non-participation: characters behave as bystanders in their own story, producing an extreme sense of alienation. (Vlasov 2024, 126)
  • Fate: death is not a reversal but a result the film has assumed from the beginning; Schrader’s “resigned sadness” rather than tragedy. (Vlasov 2024, 66, 123)
  • Lateral movement and walking: characters simply walk across the frame, treated as cinematic movement in its own right rather than as travel toward a goal.
‘The quietest scene by the sea from that summer’
Almost a Silent Movie, and the Aesthetics of Omission
  • During the sequences with Shigeru and Takako, they are in complete silence; they are indeed deaf. The late film critic Nagaharu Yodogawa, who was born in 1909 and had seen many silent movies, once said, “It almost feels like early silent films.”
  • One can praise Kitano’s direction with a phrase such as “the decisiveness of eliminating explanatory shots.” His cinematic style can be characterized by a single phrase, the Aesthetics of Omission: his refusal to fill the gaps between shots with “explanation” constitutes what amounts to a cinematic ethics.
  • A Scene at the Sea in particular, by exploiting the premise of a hearing-impaired protagonist, is often described as a high point of minimalism in Japanese cinema: emotion is conveyed through nothing more than “the crossing of gazes” and “the act of walking,” without recourse to dialogue.
  • Related to this is Kitano’s technique of never directly filming moments of accident or death. Shigeru’s death is conveyed through a surfboard left on the sand: opaque, but decisive. Vlasov reads the same logic as a general editing principle: Kitano removes the decisive moment and shows only cause and result, so that the event feels like something already settled in advance. (Vlasov 2024, 122)
  • Many scenes featuring events unrelated to the main story are present. These scenes depict bustling activity and commotion that contrast sharply with the quietude of Shigeru and Takako. There are also scenes in which the boisterous characters seem to watch over, and perhaps even admire, the deaf couple: when they gaze at them, they become quiet and composed like the couple. The contrast accentuates the quietness and stillness of their world.
  • There are many scenes with horizontal lines (the horizon, seawalls, etc.), and the movie is also filled with sideways movements, beginning with surfing, and there are abrupt falling movements: surfers into the waves, runners on the beach, a bicycle into the bay, and possibly Shigeru into the sea off screen.
Almost a Silent Movie
Time moves slower for Shigeru and Takako
  • Even though the pace of the movie is slow, time seems to move even slower for Shigeru and Takako. They miss the heat that Shigeru is supposed to compete in while they quietly gaze at the sea. In this slow world, Kitano’s camera often captures Shigeru gazing at the sea, as if he is somehow aware of his destiny to become one with it: not a march toward a destination, but an unconscious gait toward fate.
  • The missed heat is not a small joke. Vlasov uses the same scene to show how information travels in this film: we never see the disqualification. Shigeru sits on the beach watching the other entrants, and only in the following scene, through the small talk of surfers he knows, do we learn that he failed to hear his name called and was disqualified. (Vlasov 2024, 25)
  • My favorite scene is the one where Takako is facing a man doing the Radio Calisthenics, which is traditionally accompanied by its special music. A radio is beside the man, but it is silent (only the film’s background music is playing). The audience perceives the scene as Takako would: for her, calisthenics are always soundless. Kitano momentarily places us in Takako’s world, leading us to experience her silent world unconsciously.
  • It is also a cinematic narrative that impresses upon us the quiet world in which the two live. The calisthenics man is protective of them, and he says to Shigeru, “Surfing? Be careful!” This scene may also foreshadow Shigeru’s fate. Takako’s solitude on the beach while Shigeru is surfing frequently appears on screen, giving us a sense of Shigeru’s impending absence. As the film progresses, Takako’s smiles on the beach completely disappear, as if she knows that her time with Shigeru is limited.
Showing, not explaining

On what the actor is doing in such a shot:

“The actor does not simply refrain from acting; he becomes a presence that merely ‘exists’ within the frame, and is furthermore ‘confined’ within a shot longer than a standard static one. By this means the audience is able not only to see the protagonist’s solitude and alienation but to feel it.” — Vlasov 2024, 126 (my translation)
Their quietude and a moment in Takako’s world
Visual motifs: walking, the surfboard, the color blue
  • A feature frequently noted in Japanese critical discourse is Kitano’s repeated use of shots in which characters simply walk in a horizontal direction. While this has been likened to the style of Ozu Yasujiro, in Kitano’s case the composition is celebrated not as a “march toward a destination” but as an “unconscious gait toward fate.” In A Scene at the Sea, the repeated lateral tracking of shots in which the protagonists carry their surfboards functions not as narrative exposition but as purely cinematic movement in its own right. Vlasov counts many such shots in which the character is simply walking and the story does not advance at all. (Vlasov 2024, 42)
  • Images of Shigeru and Takako carrying a surfboard are shown frequently enough to establish a cinematic theme. The two boys who imitate Shigeru hold a surfboard in a similar way, doubling the image and enhancing its impact. After Shigeru is gone, the two boys each hold a surfboard separately, which emphasizes a change and highlights Shigeru’s absence by manipulating the visual motif (two are carrying one board or one each). In the final montage sequence, Takako is in the front, whereas Shigeru always carried the surfboard in the front before. She also gets on a surfboard (with Shigeru on the screen as a fragment of Takako’s memory or as a ghost). By placing Takako in Shigeru’s position, Kitano effectively translates a sense of change onto the screen through visible images. Now, Takako will live her life.
  • The color blue (Kitano blue) is omnipresent, seen in the sea, sky, and even the garbage truck. This pervasive blue creates a sense of a slow, quiet summer day by the sea. The interplay of color, silence, and slow speed melds into a cohesive cinematic narrative about the silent world of Shigeru and Takako.
  • This movie provides a good example of how even seemingly unrelated elements can enhance the effectiveness of cinematic expression.
The closing long take

Takako walking toward the sea runs 1 minute 23 seconds. Vlasov sets it beside the famous opening of Touch of Evil (1958), which at 3 minutes 43 seconds is nearly three times longer and yet feels shorter:

“In A Scene at the Sea the camera does not move, the shot is filmed in long shot, and the actress’s action within the frame does not push the story forward (she is only walking toward the sea), so the shot comes to feel like a photograph. Its effect is to stretch time and to create a meditative atmosphere.” — Vlasov 2024, 42 (my translation)
No dramatic skeleton: the story as fragments
What happens, and why that is the point
  • Vlasov’s summary of the film is deliberately flat, because the film offers nothing else to report: a garbage collector is drawn to the sea, finds a surfboard by chance, resolves to change his life, devotes his free time to surfing, makes friends among the surfers, wins a competition, and dies while surfing. (Vlasov 2024, 20)
  • Measured against conventional narrative cinema, the film is composed of “scraps” of plot with almost no causal link between scenes. Kitano’s films of this period give the impression of separate short films assembled side by side. (Vlasov 2024, 20–21)
  • Two remarks by the critic Yamane Sadao, quoted by Vlasov, set the terms for the whole week.
“As for the story, it amounts to no more than a young man working part-time on a garbage truck who picks up a broken surfboard and becomes absorbed in surfing; there is nothing that could properly be called a plot.” — Yamane Sadao (1990–1999 reviews, 85), quoted in Vlasov 2024, 21
“Both in the unfolding of the story and in the individual scenes, everything that would ordinarily count as dramatic has been stripped away.” — Yamane Sadao (1990–1999 reviews, 159), quoted in Vlasov 2024, 21

Discussion: if nothing is dramatized, what holds our attention for ninety minutes? Rhythm, repetition of images, and the sea itself are the candidates worth testing against the film.

Solitude: given, not caused
Deafness as the exception that shows the rule
  • Shigeru treats the sea as the one thing that matters and holds to it to the end, even though his employer and Takako both support him. His deafness cuts him off physically as well as socially, so the isolation is doubled. (Vlasov 2024, 124)
  • But Vlasov’s claim is that this scripted motivation is unusual for Kitano, not typical of him. Most of his films offer no explanation at all.
“The characters in Kitano’s films are indifferent to external stimulus, and give the impression of having been solitary from birth. Solitude in Kitano’s cinema can therefore be said to be ontological, that is, existential, in nature. In films such as BROTHER and A Scene at the Sea, the characters’ solitude is accounted for by elements of the screenplay, such as a hearing impairment or a language barrier, whereas in the great majority of Kitano’s films no such explanatory element is present at all. Whether they are alone in a scene or surrounded by friends, they are always depicted as beings made to feel their solitude.” — Vlasov 2024, 125–126 (my translation)
  • Solitude, on this reading, is not a narrative element but “a central concept that defines the very existence of the characters”. (Vlasov 2024, 125)
  • Question for us: does the film’s deafness therefore explain Shigeru, or does it merely give the audience a permissible reason for a solitude Kitano would have staged anyway?
Fate, and the ending we are not shown
“Kitano’s characters have no interest in life. For them death holds no fear.” (Vlasov 2024, 121)
  • Shigeru pays no attention to his job, and we are never told what he would do if dismissed. “This is a film in which Shigeru tries to connect with the sea, and his death is the natural result of that.” (Vlasov 2024, 121)
  • Vlasov then raises a hypothesis, and marks its weakness himself, which is why it is worth putting to the class rather than settling.
“Why should Shigeru, who by the final part of the film has become a skilled surfer, lose his life in so calm a sea? One hypothesis is that he was driven by an impulse to become one, for ever, with the sea he revered, and took his own life. To accept such a hypothesis is closer to treating a wish as a reality. … Given the obsessive love for the sea the film depicts, it may be inferred that even if someone had warned him he had almost no chance of surviving that day, he would probably have taken the risk and gone in.” — Vlasov 2024, 121–122 (my translation)
  • Elsewhere he puts it more flatly: Shigeru is “progressively infected by a fanatical attachment to the sea,” never shows any sign of fearing it, and has even stopped going to work, so that his death is “the logical conclusion of this fervour.” (Vlasov 2024, 65)
  • How fate is filmed, not stated: the shot begins seconds before a character enters and holds after they leave, so that the world visibly does not need them. Vlasov calls this the empty frame. Combined with cause-and-result editing that deletes the decisive moment, it makes events feel settled in advance. (Vlasov 2024, 122)
  • The effect Kitano is after is not tragedy but what Schrader calls resigned sadness: an irreversible outcome the audience has expected from the opening. (Vlasov 2024, 66, 123)
The means: performance, shot, dialogue
Acting: the actor as “model”
“The characters in Kitano’s films look like mannequins; they are close to the actor as ‘model’ in Bresson’s sense. In A Scene at the Sea, for instance, there are two scenes in which Takako sheds tears. What the audience sees, however, is no more than a single tear running down her cheek: no expression of sadness or suffering can be seen at all, and her face remains impassive.” — Vlasov 2024, 126 (my translation)

Suppressed expression lowers the dramatic register and redirects attention from momentary feeling to bodily reaction and attitude. Characters who show nothing and take part in nothing become bystanders in their own story, watching it much as we do. (Vlasov 2024, 126)
