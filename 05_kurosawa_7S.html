Introduction
Akira Kurosawa and cinematic motion

Akira Kurosawa (1910–1998) is famous for a bold, exaggerated style. Seven Samurai (1954) tells of a farming village that hires seven masterless samurai to defend it against marauding bandits. Our focus is Kurosawa’s conviction that a motion picture should be composed entirely of motion.

  • Exaggerated expression: a heightened, “Shakespearean” use of hyperbole in cinematic terms (Kiarostami).
  • Motion everywhere: multiple cameras, telephoto lenses, and a moving camera—“no shot that does not have motion” (Richie, p. 103).
  • Nature as drama: heavy rain, wind, and fire intensify emotion, culminating in the climactic rain battle.
  • Rain in cinema: a comparative look, including Tarkovsky’s Sculpting in Time.
Kurosawa and exaggeration in cinematic expression
Kurosawa’s expressive style

Kurosawa’s style is known for its use of exaggerated expression. He often employs multiple cameras, sometimes with a telephoto lens. The cameras themselves are actively moved during filming. Exaggerated natural elements, such as heavy rain, powerful wind, or large fires, are frequently used to enhance the emotional and dramatic impact. Filmmaker Abbas Kiarostami once compared Kurosawa’s style to Shakespeare’s use of hyperbole in literature, describing Kurosawa’s style as “Shakespearean” (p.c.).

Richie sharpens the same intuition in formal terms. Kurosawa, he says, “insisted that the motion-picture be composed entirely of motion. The film opens with fast pans of the bandits riding over hills, and ends with the chaos of the battle itself, motion so swift we can almost not see it at all. There is no shot that does not have motion, either in the object photographed, or in the movement of the camera itself” (Richie, p. 103). The exaggeration is not decorative; it is the medium.

Cinematic techniques in Seven Samurai

Besides the final battle in the hyperbolic rain (which we will address in the next slide), several memorable moments in Seven Samurai showcase Kurosawa’s use of varied cinematic techniques.

  • The opening sequence with the raiders on horseback: Kurosawa employs dynamic camera movements, including sweeping shots and close-ups. Telephoto lenses are used to keep wide areas in focus. This sequence follows the title roll, which has a sound effect of drumbeats. The drumbeats turn into the hoofbeats as the raiders’ sequence begins, suturing sound into the next image without a seam.
Recruitment, Kikuchiyo, and the multi-camera setup
Building characters, capturing performance
  • The recruitment of the samurai: Kurosawa uses long takes and effective framing to develop the character of each samurai as he is encountered. Richie observes that the sequence is built as a “mosaic of tiny scenes” held together with wipes, and that we get to know each samurai through how they react to a small, often comic test (Richie, p. 103).
  • Kikuchiyo speaking to the farmers: the scene showcases Kurosawa’s use of montage and Toshiro Mifune’s improvisation, adding depth to Kikuchiyo’s character. Richie notes that what is most powerful in the scene is that Mifune acts directly into the camera, and that “each shot of Mifune is a bit shorter than the one which went before. The tempo is accelerated by the cutting, and these long scenes become shorter” (Richie, p. 105).
  • Up to eight cameras were used to capture a scene from different angles simultaneously. Kurosawa noted that with multiple cameras he did not have to stop the actors and have them repeat for shots from different angles. Using multiple cameras, Kurosawa was able to capture the actors’ fluid performances unbroken, then assemble the rhythm of impact in the cutting room.
The rain battle sequence
Life and death in Seven Samurai’s rain

The final battle scene in the rain in Seven Samurai is one of the most famous sequences in cinema. Kurosawa made sure it would be highly expressive. He used special effects, splashing water upward from the ground in certain spots, and mixed black ink into the splashed water. Usually these effects are the main focus when people discuss his exaggerated style. But what is achieved in terms of cinematic narrative? What makes the rain sequence so memorable?

The director Andrei Tarkovsky, himself known for his use of rain, points to a single image in the sequence in which the narrative of life and death is fully realized as an image, without recourse to symbolism.

And a shot from The Seven Samurai. A mediaeval Japanese village. A fight is going on between some horsemen, and the samurai who are on foot. It is pouring with rain, there is mud everywhere. The samurai wear an ancient Japanese garment which leaves most of the leg bare, and their legs are plastered with mud. And when one samurai falls down dead we see the rain washing away the mud and his leg becoming white, as white as marble. A man is dead; that is an image which is a fact. It is innocent of symbolism, and that is an image. — Andrei Tarkovsky, Sculpting in Time, p. 73

Tarkovsky’s point is that the sequence is powerful because the narrative of life and death concerning the samurai is fully realized on screen as an image, not relayed by a symbol. Earlier in the same chapter he names the criterion: “the purity of cinema, its inherent strength, is revealed not in the symbolic aptness of images however bold these may be, but in the capacity of those images to express a specific, unique, actual fact” (Tarkovsky, p. 72). The white leg under washing rain meets that criterion exactly.

Rain in cinema: Tarkovsky
Rain as material setting, not as symbol

In the same book, Tarkovsky talks about the presentation of rain in his own films. Audiences keep asking what the rain means, and he keeps refusing the question.

Rain is after all typical of the landscape in which I grew up; in Russia you have those long, dreary, persistent rains. … Rain, fire, water, snow, dew, the driving ground wind, all are part of the material setting in which we dwell; I would even say of the truth of our lives. I am therefore puzzled when I am told that people cannot simply enjoy watching nature, when it is lovingly reproduced on the screen, but have to look for some hidden meaning they feel it must contain. — Tarkovsky, Sculpting in Time, p. 212

A page later he sharpens the distinction. Rain in his films is not “bad weather,” nor is it “a symbol of something else.” He uses it, rather, to “create a particular aesthetic setting in which to steep the action of the film” (Tarkovsky, p. 213). The rain belongs to the actual world the camera records; it gains its force from being unmistakably there.

The intensity of rain in Tarkovsky’s films is different from that of Kurosawa’s. But how Tarkovsky thinks rain contributes to cinematic narrative in Seven Samurai, and how he uses it in his own films, are similar. A familiar natural phenomenon, like rain, becomes effective for enriching cinematic narrative when it is immediate and complete on screen, without symbolizing anything that would distract the audience’s awareness.

Rain in cinema: a comparative look
Singin’ in the Rain (1952) by Kelly and Donen

After Don (Gene Kelly) has fallen in love with Kathy, we see his happy and ecstatic dancing in the rain. Usually we avoid exposing ourselves to the rain and getting wet. Don enjoys being wet here. What does the rain do in this sequence (even in the title of the movie itself)? Do you see anything else besides the rain itself that makes this sequence effective?

Blade Runner (1982) by Ridley Scott

A Blade Runner, an officer whose task is the extermination of replicants (artificial human beings), pursues Roy Batty, the replicant leader, in a brutal chase. Roy, whose lifespan is limited to four years, has Deckard at his mercy, hanging from a high building edge by one grip. Instead of killing him, Roy saves his life. Standing in the downpour, Roy delivers his final monologue: “all those moments will be lost in time, like tears in rain” before dying. The rain and tears are present, but also the white dove. Evaluate the effect of the sequence based on what we can see in it.

Rain in cinema: a comparative look
The Matrix (1999) by the Wachowskis: Neo vs. Agent Smith in rain

I feel that the rain battle scene between Neo and Agent Smith in The Matrix is somehow an homage to Kurosawa. There is even a character named Captain Mifune in The Matrix, while the actor Toshiro Mifune played Kikuchiyo in Seven Samurai, who dies in the rain.
