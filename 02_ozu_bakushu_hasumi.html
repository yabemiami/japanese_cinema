Introduction
Why Early Summer, and why read it with Hasumi?

Early Summer (1951, 麦秋) is usually regarded as the film in which Ozu’s style is most fully realized. We use it as a case study, paired with Shigehiko Hasumi’s Directed by Yasujiro Ozu (UC Press, 2024), to shift the question from “what is Ozu’s style?” to “what does that style reveal about cinema itself?”

Hasumi’s claim runs through the whole session: cinema has no “grammar,” only limits, the things it physically cannot show. Most films stay watchable by concealing those limits; Ozu instead exposes them, which is why Hasumi calls him “among the freest of filmmakers” (HS, p. 151). His constraint is not poverty of expression but freedom from the obligation to hide.

What this session traces
  • The obsessions, reframed — space (low camera, frames within frames), people (centered figures, crossing the 180° line), and time (no fades; finches and clocks as indices of what cinema cannot record).
  • The eye vs. the look — the camera captures eyes but never the gaze itself, and Ozu’s “off” conversation cuts lay this limit bare instead of papering over it.
  • Holding still — the lyricism of parallel gazes at an offscreen object, the camera placed on a moving body, and the family photograph that quietly stages separation and death.
  • Watching differently — “things Ozuesque” as social reassurance vs. actually seeing the films, with kindred auteurs (Sembène, Akerman, Kaurismäki) as an extension.

Throughline: constraint as freedom, and what Hasumi calls “movement in stasis” (P, p. 5) — learning to watch a film as more than the “space between events.”

Ozu’s obsession with his aesthetics
Ozu’s stylistic approaches:

Early Summer was directed by Yasujiro Ozu in 1951. We can see Ozu’s fully developed style here; it is often considered to have most of his cinematic styles realized in this film.

Hasumi (2024) argues that, far from being a stylist who narrowed his vocabulary, Ozu should be considered “among the freest of filmmakers” precisely because he devotes himself to constraint and in doing so makes the limits of cinema itself conspicuous (HS, p. 151). What he “sacrificed” were not means of self-expression but the constraints cinema imposes (HS, p. 151).

Ozu’s aesthetic styles:

Ozu is known for his stylistic obsessions about how things are placed on the screen.

  1. The low placement of the camera, as if the observer were sitting on a tatami mat with the character in the movie.
  2. The characters in conversation are placed in the center of the shot in a uniform manner, disregarding the Hollywood grammar (the imaginary line for the camera placement).
  3. Ozu’s film has many shots but no fadeout effects to suggest the passing of time. Ozu uses regular shots of things to indicate this.
  4. The famous Koinobori sequence (fish banners): we just don’t know what is going on (and the bride procession: they say they see it, but where is it?).

Hasumi calls the underlying logic “movement in stasis”: a shot may appear static, but its plurality of elements “might at any moment bring stark tensions to life, slicing at the eye or disorienting the look” (P, p. 5).

Ozu’s aesthetic styles: space, people, time

Ozu is known for his stylistic obsessions about how things are placed on the screen.

1. Space
  • The low placement of the camera, as if the observer were sitting on a tatami mat with the character in the movie. The soles of the actors’ feet are shown; it is said Ozu made sure they were freshly white tabi (Japanese socks).
  • Ozu also seems to enjoy creating frames within the screen frame. In the living room below, two sets of sliding paper walls and a set of paper blinds create a three-layered frame; he often uses anything available in each scene to construct these space-frames.
2. People
  • The characters are placed in the center of the shot in a uniform manner, disregarding how the conversation is depicted in Hollywood movies of the time (the imaginary line that the observer would not cross over).
  • Hasumi notes this was a deliberate policy, not an oversight: Ozu’s editor Yoshiyasu Hamamura is known to have raised the 180-degree-rule issue with him, but Ozu did not change course (HS, p. 154). As Truffaut put it, the cuts “give an impression of being strangely off” (HS, p. 153).
3. Time
  • Ozu’s film has many shots but no fadeout effects to suggest the passing of time. In Bakushu, Ozu uses shots of finches in a cage instead.
  • Hasumi reminds us that time itself, as the flow of duration, is something cinema cannot record (HS, p. 152). The finches, the clock, the wind in branches: these become indirect indices of an invisible subject the camera cannot reach. Where other directors paper over the limit with fades, Ozu lets it show as another image.
4. Ozu’s indifference towards the plot continuity: story
Ozu’s aesthetic styles: where is that?

Ozu is known for his stylistic obsessions. Sometimes he goes beyond the plot continuity in a conventional way.

  • The famous Koinobori sequence (traditional fish banners): we are left uncertain about what is happening. The mother looks to her left, but the open space in the direction of her gaze is never established in the film. The next shot shows Koinobori in the sky. Neither the window in that direction, the Koinobori banners, nor the occasion (Children’s Day on May 5th, when the banners are raised for children’s well-being) have been introduced into the plot. The scene even feels eerie.
  • For Hasumi this eeriness is structural rather than incidental. Ozu uses an offscreen object as a hinge: two figures suddenly fix their eyes on the same point and a lyricism emerges “through the simple gesture of two people fixing their eyes on the same object at the same time” (HS, p. 159). The Koinobori, the bridal procession winding through the fields in Tokyo Story, the balloon in Early Summer: each belongs to this family of unannounced offscreen anchors.
Hasumi 2024: pp. 149–158
No Grammar, but Concealment of the Limitations
  • Cinema does not have a “grammar” that regulates forms of expression as natural language does. There are no (un)grammatical movies. It only has limits (physical impossibilities): things that cannot be shown on the screen, and these very limits define what cinema is.
  • Hasumi sharpens the point: a filmmaker has only three kinds of camerawork available (traveling shot, axis-rotation pan/tilt, static shot), and there are no absolute principles of editing dictating how they should be joined (HS, p. 150). Cinema, he writes, is “an environment thoroughly devoid of freedom” (HS, p. 150).
  • Limitations: the events in our reality are perceived in a totality (within the limits of what our senses can perceive). Events that cinema depicts are always presented with less than we have in the real world, in fragments and always with less information.
  • While conventional films keep the audience “successfully” entertained, this does not mean that filmmakers follow a “cinematic grammar”; they are simply skilled at concealing these limits. The freest of films, conversely, are those “that devote themselves to constraint and that in doing so are able to make the limits of cinema itself conspicuous” (HS, p. 151).
Different ways to watch films
Different ways to watch films:

We are somewhat conditioned to believe that being entertained by a movie often is to perceive it as close to reality. But reality and the reality of cinema are two different kinds. Some film directors achieve that facade by concealing the limitations of cinema.

You are the cohort, as well, of this whole facade, or even this reality conspiracy, in that you accept cinema’s intention to be realistic without consciously examining it. Actually the building of this facade cannot be complete without the audience cooperating.

It is in a similar way that we believe there is a standard English, which is actually just the prescriptive grammar we are told to accept as the correct way to talk in school. But there are many ways to speak English differently. You also unconsciously train yourself in which ways movies are supposed to be watched. But there are many different ways to watch and make movies.

Hasumi’s Prologue: “things Ozuesque” vs. seeing the film
  • For Hasumi, “things Ozuesque” are a “gratuitous lubricant”, a risk-free social game of mutual reassurance that “never leads people toward awkward silences or messy disagreements” (P, p. 1). Crucially: “The reason everyone thinks they know Ozu … is because no one really sees Ozu’s films” (P, p. 4).
  • Real watching is what Hasumi calls the “pleasure of erasure” reversed: a “bourgeois (wordly or common) virtue” we usually practice keeps the Ozuesque comfortable and the films unseen (P, p. 5). To look at Ozu seriously is to give that virtue up.
Exposing the Limitations
Ozu’s Exposure of Cinematic Limitation
  • In a sense, conventional auteurs are constrained by these cinematic limitations and are not free from them. Ozu, however, exposes cinema’s limitations and frees himself from the need to hide them.
  • For example, while the camera can capture an actor’s eyes, it can never capture the gaze itself, the look their eyes create. This is a fundamental limitation of cinema. Hasumi calls this the “cruel schism between the eye and the look”: the eye is a visible object, but looking is not (HS, p. 152).
  • Even in a well-crafted conversation scene, the exact eye lines between two people talking to each other cannot be perfectly captured (It is easiler to do so in the real space). Cinema can only surrender whenever confronted with two sets of eyes looking at each other (HS, p. 156): spatial simultaneity has to be replaced by temporal succession (a 180° pan, or a shot/reverse shot).
  • As a result, editing and shooting techniques, such as reverse shots, are designed to conceal this limitation. However, Ozu’s approach seems to expose it. The eeriness we feel when watching the conversation sequences in Ozu’s films makes us wonder why that is. This, in turn, makes some of us notice how conversation scenes are constructed in films.
Truffaut on the strange Ozu space
“Ordinarily, when using a shot/reverse shot setup to film a conversation … you cut back and forth from the same side of the subjects. … But in Ozu films the camera seems to film one character from this side one minute … and the next minute we cut to the other character filmed from the opposite side. This is not just an impression. It must have been an intentional directorial choice, and it creates the uneasy sensation that, if you were to follow a character’s eyeline, there might not be anyone there on the other end, that every time we have a cut, the other person ceases to be there.” — François Truffaut, interview with Hasumi & Yamada, 1980 (HS, pp. 153–54)
Liberation from Limits / The Impossibility of the Gaze
Ozu’s Liberation from Cinematic Limitations
  • Rather than pretending to capture the unattainable gaze on screen by strictly following the imaginary line, Ozu focuses on the actors’ eyes during conversations, accepting this cinematic impossibility. His interest lies elsewhere. He positions the actors’ eyes in the same spot on the screen with the same gaze direction (sometimes symmetrically opposite), even as the shots reverse between them. Ozu creates a compositional pattern based on the characters’ eyes. He does not try to capture something that is unattainable. He does not even pretend to do so.
  • Hasumi identifies two fictions Ozu’s direct-to-camera staring lays bare (HS, pp. 155–56):
    1. People, especially Japanese people, do not in everyday life peer into each other’s eyes the way Ozu’s characters do. The unnaturalness is a “concocted fiction”.
    2. Cinema itself had to concoct a second fiction because two sets of eyes meeting cannot be captured in a single static shot. Editing then poses as if mutual eyelines were natural.
The Impossibility of Capturing the Gaze
  • Hasumi (2024: p. 156) points out that the reverse shot technique based on the imaginary line was originally developed to depict psychological convergence in romantic melodramas and to intensify conflict in action films. It is, ultimately, a cinematic invention. Ozu’s reverse shots, organized by screen composition, are also an invention. But his approach is driven by his obsession with compositional organization on the screen, not by dramatization à la Hollywood.
  • His stylistic choices, such as crossing the imaginary line to establish compositional continuity in a conversation sequence, challenge the boundaries of cinematic expression. He ventures into a space where a conversation sequence comes close to being perceived not as a conversation, but as a series of repetitive images. The perceptive audiences, who were not completely tricked by the fabrication of the look, would have leniency towards Ozu’s cinematic approach to conversation scenes, and rather get stimulated for something different.
  • An extreme case: in Floating Weeds, Ganjirô Nakamura and Machiko Kyô “stare each other down from under the eaves of facing houses during a downpour” (HS, p. 157). The density of the scene comes not from psychological conflict but from the overwhelming tension created by the meeting of glares: we sense that if it lasted a moment longer, “cinema might henceforth cease to be cinema”.
Side by Side: a lyricism without symbolism
The opposite case: relaxation through parallel gazes

Alongside the tension of mismatched eyelines, Hasumi identifies a counter-movement in Ozu: a “relaxation phenomenon” in which intimate figures sit side by side, often diagonal to the camera, and let their gazes converge on an offscreen object (HS, pp. 158–59).

Examples Hasumi works through
  • Early Summer, hotel-tea scene: Setsuko Hara, Chikage Awashima and two married friends sit across a table; teasing “parallel displacement” ends with the four arranged diagonally, eyelines meeting only obliquely (HS, p. 158).
  • Late Autumn, lakeside café: mother and daughter eat azuki beans, then together turn to look at Mount Haruna through the window. “The motion itself evokes a sense of empathy that a cut between two sets of eyes would not convey” (HS, p. 159).
  • Early Summer, Ochanomizu café: Hara and Hiroshi Nihon’yanagi turn together to a painting on the wall as he begins to speak of her brother who died at war.
  • Early Summer, Tokyo National Museum garden: a balloon breaks free and the elderly couple follow it with their eyes in “choreographed synchrony” (HS, p. 159). Later, back in their hometown, they watch a bridal procession winding through the fields.
  • Early Summer, Yamato house: Chieko Higashiyama and Ichirô Sugai sit together to watch a bride parade (HS, p. 161).
  • There Was a Father: father and son fishing barefoot at the riverside, casting their lines back upstream in mechanically mirrored motion. Lyricism, Hasumi writes, reaches its highest expression through backs that exhibit exceptional eloquence without so much as a hint of movement (HS, p. 161).

The unifying claim: Ozu’s lyricism is dynamic, never contained by the static properties of the image, and arrives “simply through the gesture of two people fixing their eyes on the same object at the same time” (HS, p. 159), with no symbolic weight attached to the object itself.

Ozuesque Movement: making us envy motion
Holding still on something that moves
  • Even in a director famous for the static shot, Hasumi argues that “the greatest of cinematic movements can come to life in ‘holding still’” (HS, p. 170). The camera does not move; a vehicle on which it sits does, and the world streams past.
  • Late Spring & Early Summer — the Yokosuka line: Chishû Ryû and Hara, or Ryû and Seiji Miyaguchi, ride side by side on the bench seats with the landscape flowing behind them, much like the father and son fishing at the riverbank. “The outdoor scenery streams in the background behind two people seated side by side” (HS, p. 165).
  • Tokyo Story — the sightseeing bus through Marunouchi, the Imperial Palace, Ginza: Ryû, Higashiyama and Hara sit in different rows, but the “languid, almost sensual gliding movement” draws us in. “We are entranced, as if under the influence of genuine magic, and find ourselves wondering how the films of Ozu … are nonetheless able to pull off such a thing as making us envy motion” (HS, p. 168).
  • Floating Weeds — the lighthouse passing on the ferry: the same lighthouse seen earlier in static shots “slowly traverses the screen from right to left” as the boat drifts in. “The lighthouse literally drifts past. The fluidity of its gliding movement is breathtaking” (HS, p. 169).

The general formula: the subject does not move; the camera does not move; the camera is placed on a moving body (commuter train, streetcar, sightseeing bus, ferry). Stillness becomes the precondition for the most affecting motion (HS, p. 169).

Stopping to Move: holding still as ritual of separation
The family photograph in Early Summer

Near the end of Early Summer, three generations of the family pose together for a photograph in the familiar first-floor living room. “On the photographer’s instructions, they pose and hold still” (HS, p. 171). For once Ozu allows the frontal full shot he otherwise avoids: bodies face forward, eyes fix on the same point, motion ceases.

Hasumi reads this as a mirror of our own activity as viewers: “To watch a film is to share the experience of holding still and looking in the same direction as others, much as they do in posing for the camera” (HS, p. 171). They sit in cruel light; we sit in “complicit darkness.”

Photograph ↔ separation: a structural law
  • In Ozu, the group photograph always introduces a narrative of separation or death (HS, p. 172): There Was a Father (class photo, then a student drowns), Brothers and Sisters of the Toda Family (matriarch’s 60th-birthday portrait, then the patriarch’s death that evening), Record of a Tenement Gentleman, Early Summer, Late Autumn.
  • The eerie biographical echo: in Brothers and Sisters of the Toda Family the patriarch dies on his wife’s 60th birthday; Ozu himself died precisely on his own 60th birthday (December 1963) (HS, p. 174). Hasumi calls this scene “the negative center of the Ozuesque cinematic system, the point at which fiction and reality … perfectly overlap”.
  • Late Autumn’s wedding-anteroom portrait recalls the same posture. As Chishû Ryû mumbles in An Autumn Afternoon when asked whether his formal wear is for a funeral: “Something like that.” (HS, p. 178) Weddings and funerals are joined by the theme of holding still.

Hasumi closes the chapter by linking staircases and wedding photographs as twin Ozuesque devices, each ordinarily withheld and shown only when separation must be staged: one in An Autumn Afternoon, the other in Late Autumn.

Seeing through Ozu’s camera
Seeing through Ozu’s camera
  • Ozu’s acceptance of these limits, and his unique approach to conversation sequences, places us in an unfamiliar and sometimes uncomfortable position as viewers. He seems to offer us an opportunity to refresh or even renew our cinematic experiences through entirely different sensibilities.
  • Hasumi writes (2024: p. 157):
    “We give ourselves over to the flow of the narrative and abandon really looking at the screen. But where we fix our attention deliberately on the screen, our eyes succumb to uncontrollable agitation. To look at an Ozu film is to maintain the eye in a state of constant and continuously renewing agitation. This is an exceptionally cruel experience for cinema itself, since it causes a decisive moment to come closing in, a point where it seems that with even one step farther, cinema might cease to be cinema.” — Shigehiko Hasumi (HS, p. 157)
  • The Prologue gives the same idea another wording: Ozu’s films “come at our eyes with constant incitements toward change. Movement lives here in the raw, as the ever-renewing present … a movement we might also call movement in stasis” (P, p. 5). To take it seriously is to give up the comfort of the “space between events.”
  • It is like the Greek skepticism’s epoché: suspending judgement for fresh observation.
  • After these agitations, and with the rather Nietzschean “Cinema is dead” type of inspiration, we can free ourselves from the belief in the “good movie.” It is an idea rooted in Western cinema (many so-called cinematic grammars are Western conventions). Without the previous central pillar, we can invite new ways of watching and critiquing films.

When we talk about foreign films, we tend to focus mainly on subject matters and material cultures. But how movies are made, how shots are composed and edited, and even imagining how people of the time watched these films are all part of our experience of foreign cinema.

Ozunesque: with or without intention
First, distinguish: “Ozuesque” (Hasumi) vs. “Ozunesque” (our extension)

Hasumi insists that “no single Ozu film ever resembles things Ozuesque” (P, p. 4): the easy genre of social reassurance has to be set aside if we want to see the films themselves. What follows are not exemplars of “things Ozuesque” in his sense, but other auteurs whose mise-en-scène shares Ozu’s commitment to compositional pattern and to holding still.

Ousmane Sembène: Ceddo (1977)

The father of African cinema, Senegalese auteur Ousmane Sembène, demonstrates remarkable control over mise-en-scène, including the placement of people, framing with objects within the screen, and various shot/editing techniques, all of which contribute to his unique compositional style.

Chantal Akerman: Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles (1975)

Long static shots of Jeanne Dielman, a single mother, going about her daily routines in her tidy apartment quickly establish a compositional pattern on the screen. In some of these long shots, surrounded by mundane objects, the dismalness of her life is captured through her subtle unrest, conveyed by stillness and silence. Her patterned, cinematic life on screen seems to anticipate a demise, much like the dissolution of family often portrayed in Ozu’s films.

Aki Kaurismäki: The Match Factory Girl (1990)

Through long and static shots, Kaurismäki sketches the mundane life of a young woman working at a match factory. Although the film is quiet, her life is filled with misfortune and harsh circumstances. In these slow and silent cinematic spaces, she eventually transforms into a serial killer.
